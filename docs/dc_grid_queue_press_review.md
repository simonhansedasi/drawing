# Press review: data center siting vs. grid capacity

Research note supporting `civil/dc_grid_queue.ipynb` / `dc_grid_queue_post.md`. Not a formal academic literature review -- a scan of published press and one peer-reviewed study, checking whether the piece's core finding (data centers get proposed according to existing market size, not remaining grid headroom) is already covered, and whether the framing holds up against current reporting. Searched 2026-08-26.

## The core finding is well covered nationally -- and Texas just became the live example

The interconnection-queue bottleneck this piece is built on is a major, actively-reported story, not a niche indicator. As of late 2025, roughly 2,600 GW of generation and storage capacity sat stuck in US interconnection queues -- more than the country's entire installed capacity -- with data centers facing waits of up to 12 years in the worst cases and a median approaching five years nationally. PJM, the largest US grid operator, missed its reliability target for the first time in its history in its December 2025 capacity auction, 6.6 GW short, driven by data-center load growth outpacing new generation 2-to-1. ([RMI](https://rmi.org/resources/interconnection-reform-ai-data-centers-generator-queues/), [Introl](https://introl.com/blog/pjm-grid-crisis-6gw-shortfall-data-center-power-2027), [Verse](https://verse.inc/blog/the-data-center-power-queue-crisis))

**Texas specifically is not a hypothetical worst case -- it's an active policy crisis, three weeks old.** On 2026-08-03, Governor Abbott effectively paused all new data-center grid connections in Texas pending a state audit, after ERCOT's large-load interconnection queue hit roughly 474 GW -- more than 5x the state's record peak demand (85.5 GW) and more than 20x its actual generation additions since 2024 (~23 GW). ERCOT confirmed to press that the directive "effectively pauses all data center projects." The audit covers ERCOT's "Batch Zero" review of 1,800+ queued projects, 90% of them data centers. ([Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/08/03/texas-data-center-project-audit-greg-abbott/), [Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/texas-hits-pause-data-center-interconnections/827046/), [Houston Public Media](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/energy-environment/2026/08/03/558529/gov-greg-abbott-pauses-new-data-centers-until-ercot-puct-audit-energy-water-usage/))

This matters directly for the piece: the `facilities.geojson` "proposed" status for Texas's 25 sites is a live number that may already be moving as the state works through its audit -- worth a line noting the caption describes a snapshot, not a settled state. Texas Tribune separately counts 335 operating data centers in Texas (vs. this piece's 224 total facilities of all statuses) -- a real discrepancy, likely because Tribune's count and merascope's 4-source merge use different inclusion criteria; not something to paper over if this framing gets used publicly again.

## A closely related peer-reviewed study exists, and it refines rather than duplicates this finding

NYU Tandon (Nature Cities, 2026) analyzed 4,283 US data centers and found 97.5% sit inside metro/micropolitan areas, contradicting the "remote rural warehouse" narrative. Their top predictor of location was **electricity infrastructure availability** -- proximity to already-built substations and transmission, including a specific pattern of new data centers colonizing shuttered coal-plant sites for their leftover grid connections. ([techxplore summary](https://techxplore.com/news/2026-07-power-grids-centers-cities.html))

This is not the same claim as this piece's finding, and the difference is worth stating explicitly if the piece gets extended: NYU measured proximity to *built* infrastructure (substations, transmission lines physically present); this piece measures *remaining headroom* (EIA-860M's planned-vs-operating ratio). The two aren't in tension -- they're plausibly the same mechanism seen from two sides. A place accumulates data centers because the grid infrastructure is already there to tap into (NYU's finding) -- and precisely because so many facilities have already tapped into it, headroom for the *next* one keeps shrinking (this piece's finding). Built-infrastructure presence and remaining-capacity scarcity are two faces of the same saturated-market coin. Worth citing the NYU study by name if this piece is developed further -- it's the closest legitimate academic backing for the market-momentum argument, not a competing claim.

## Oklahoma's real-world numbers independently back the share-normalized finding

This piece's share-normalized check found Oklahoma leads the country in proposed-DC share (13 of 39 facilities, 33%) despite a mid-pack grid_capacity_score. Real reporting corroborates this as a genuine, currently unfolding hotspot, not noise: 7 of the 10 most congested points on the entire Southwest Power Pool grid were in Oklahoma as of summer 2024, residential electricity prices rose ~7% year-over-year through Sept 2025 tied to the data-center boom, and Oklahoma's own state regulators have publicly warned that "the data center boom could lead utilities to overestimate demand based on speculative proposals and unverified projections." ([Oklahoma Voice](https://oklahomavoice.com/2026/08/06/some-data-centers-are-building-their-own-power-plants-in-oklahoma-most-want-to-plug-into-the-grid/), [Read Frontier](https://www.readfrontier.org/stories/oklahomas-data-center-boom-is-about-to-hit-the-grid-and-your-power-bill/))

## The broader "market momentum over grid capacity" mechanism is a named phenomenon in trade press

Data Center Knowledge and Rice Business Wisdom both describe the same pattern this piece quantifies: established hubs (Ashburn, VA in particular) keep attracting new builds through path dependency -- sunk network infrastructure, established permitting relationships, proximity benefits for interconnected workloads -- even as those same hubs become the most grid-constrained (Northern Virginia's average interconnection wait is now up to 7 years). The trade press also documents the beginning of a countertrend: roughly 67% of newly planned data centers are now sited in rural areas as developers hunt "stranded power" in less-saturated grids -- explicitly naming Idaho, Louisiana, and Oklahoma as emerging destinations for exactly that reason. ([Data Center Knowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/data-center-site-selection/finding-strength-in-numbers-the-data-center-clustering-effect), [Rice Business Wisdom](https://business.rice.edu/wisdom/what-really-drives-data-center-location-decisions))

One methodological caveat worth carrying forward: UC Berkeley economist Severin Borenstein has publicly flagged that interconnection-queue figures are likely inflated by developers filing duplicate requests across multiple utility territories to "queue-shop" -- a critique aimed at raw interconnection-request GW totals specifically, not at EIA-860M's planned-vs-operating ratio (a different data source, aggregated differently), so it likely doesn't undermine this piece's `grid_capacity_score` directly, but it's a live, credible skepticism about the broader numbers this beat runs on and worth knowing about if `iso_queue_mw` framing gets used more heavily later. ([Broadband Breakfast](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/economist-warns-data-center-interconnection-requests-may-be-inflated/))

## ERCOT's own grid governance is the documented common cause of both halves of the Texas finding (2026-08-26 addition)

Simon's own read, checked against reporting rather than assumed: Texas isn't at both extremes (most raw proposals, worst `grid_capacity_score`) by coincidence of size -- it's one real mechanism causing both. ERCOT runs a "connect-and-manage" interconnection process that is genuinely faster than "the more cumbersome interconnection studies required in many other regional grids" (PJM/Virginia included), and Texas's deregulated market lets developers negotiate directly with generators via bilateral PPAs instead of going through a vertically-integrated utility -- both cited by legal/industry analysts as the specific draw for data-center developers. ([Davis Graham](https://davisgraham.com/news-events/texas-and-ercot-the-structural-advantage-for-data-center-power/), [Build Inc.](https://build.inc/insights/texas-data-centers-ercot-grid))

That same speed advantage is what let the queue overrun capacity: ERCOT's large-load study process was built for roughly 40-50 projects at a time and is now processing 1,800+, about 90% of them data centers, totaling the 474 GW that triggered the Abbott pause. PUCT approved a new "batch" study process specifically to restore the speed advantage without losing control of the queue. ([GovTech Insider](https://insider.govtech.com/texas/news/texas-approves-new-ercot-process-to-speed-data-center-grid-connections), [KeenTel](https://keentelengineering.com/ercot-interconnection-surge-ai-load))

This is a sharper causal claim than "market size predicts the count" (still true, and still the better statistical predictor across the full 48-state panel) -- it specifically explains *why Texas* sits at both extremes simultaneously, which market size alone does not. Worth leading with this mechanism over the size-based one if the piece gets developed further; it's the one actually documented as the cause, not just correlated with the outcome.

## Bottom line

This piece's finding is not novel in the "does this happen" sense -- it's a well-documented, actively-unfolding national story, and Texas is its current flashpoint in real time. What this piece adds that the press coverage above doesn't: a quantified, sourced, reproducible number for the market-size-vs-grid-capacity relationship (rho=+0.79 vs. rho=+0.01) across all 48 states from a real merged national dataset, not just narrative description of individual hotspots. That's a legitimate, citable contribution on top of well-covered ground, not a rediscovery -- worth saying so plainly rather than implying originality the underlying mechanism doesn't have. The ERCOT-specific mechanism above is the sharper, better explanation for Texas itself and should lead over the generic market-size framing where the two are in tension.
