Ninety years later, the redlining map still lines up [until I get hired]

Last post: physical infrastructure (grid access, water, seismic risk, and so on) explains 62% of the real environmental-justice index across Washington ZIP codes -- but mostly through one underlying urban-density axis, not five independent causes, and it breaks badly on both a poor rural reservation and a rich urban neighborhood. This post checks something that isn't infrastructure at all: an explicitly discriminatory 90-year-old policy map, against that same real EJ index.

In the 1930s the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation graded neighborhoods in major US cities A ("Best") through D ("Hazardous") for mortgage lending -- the actual origin of the word "redlining." Washington had exactly three cities big enough to get surveyed: Seattle, Tacoma, Spokane. Everywhere else in the state was never graded at all. Overlaid those 90-year-old maps against the same real EJ index the last post used -- environmental hazard exposure x demographic vulnerability, EPA's own formula, matched at neighborhood resolution.

Formerly redlined ("D") neighborhoods average 96% higher present-day EJ-index burden than the "Best" ("A") neighborhoods graded in the same cities. Holds in all three cities individually (Seattle, Tacoma, and Spokane each clear statistical significance on their own, not one dragging the average). Overall: rho = 0.46 across 159 neighborhoods, p < 0.0000001.

One more check before trusting that number: HOLC's own criteria explicitly penalized proximity to industry, and the environmental half of the same EJ index is industrial/pollution proximity -- so this could just be a land-use artifact, redlined areas were near factories then and factories don't move much. Split the index back into its two halves to check. It isn't: grade correlates with today's demographic composition at rho=0.47, but with today's pollution-proximity alone at only rho=0.24, and the gap between "Best" and "Hazardous" neighborhoods on that specific measure has nearly closed (1.05x, versus 1.84x on the demographic side). Ninety years erased most of the industrial channel. What survived is the demographic one -- a segregation-legacy finding, not a zoning-persistence one.

Not claiming this as new -- redlining's link to present-day inequality is well documented nationally. The actual question was whether Washington's own data shows the same thing other cities' data has shown, against a properly built EJ index rather than a demographic proxy, and for the reason the literature actually claims. It does.

One more thing worth closing on. The physical/urban-density axis from the last post -- the one that explains 36% of this same real EJ index across the whole state -- explains under 1% of it once you're only comparing neighborhoods inside these three cities. Every HOLC-graded neighborhood was already about as urban as Washington gets, so there's no rural-to-urban gap left for that number to find. Redlining grade explains 18% here instead -- almost exactly the gap physical geography leaves on the table at this zoom level. Not a competing explanation. Two honest answers to the same question, depending entirely on how far out you're standing.
