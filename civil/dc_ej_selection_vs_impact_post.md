Visualizing the unseen (until I get hired): Selection or impact?

Last time: ZIP codes hosting a data center score worse on the Environmental Justice index, even controlling for density. Obvious next question: are data centers landing where things were already worse, or making them worse?

No dataset I have records when a site opened, so a clean per-site before-and-after isn't possible. But the demographic half of the index comes from the Census, which goes back. Pulled 2013 ZIP-level estimates and compared to today's: 87% of the current gap already existed in 2013, a decade before the recent boom. Selection, not impact, is the dominant story.

Extended that to all 12 years back to 2011: the gap sits flat for nine straight years, then climbs in exactly the three years the Census itself warns not to compare cleanly, a COVID-era weighting change. Chased it down instead of citing it: the correction actually pushes poverty DOWN, wrong direction to explain a rise; the COVID years aren't less reliable by margin of error; and the "clean" 2011-2019 baseline was never actually verified, real response rates fell 97.6% to 92% over that stretch, unflagged but real. Doesn't prove the rise is an artifact, does mean I can't rule it out, so the chart says so, a halo scaled to each year's real nonresponse rate instead of a clean line pretending otherwise.

Split the index into its two ingredients, poverty and minority share, and poverty had been quietly falling all decade, matching real national history, nothing to do with COVID. Hosting ZIPs' poverty fell faster, though: are minorities being lifted out of poverty near data centers? Tested it with race-specific Census tables instead of guessing. No. Within each race, hosting and non-hosting ZIPs sit at parity. What's different is the racial mix, 10-13 points more minority residents in hosting ZIPs, and minority poverty runs structurally higher everywhere in the country. Composition, not economic effect.

Formalized that into a decomposition and it turned up something bigger: whether hosting ZIPs are poorer at all depends on how you average ZIP codes. Weight every ZIP equally and the gap is real, +1.3 points in 2011 narrowing to +0.6 by 2022. Weight every person equally, the more standard choice, and it's parity, sometimes hosting even ahead. Same data, two honest conventions, two answers.

That's Simpson's Paradox: pool two populations at a different mix and a trend appears that isn't inside either alone. White residents only, near parity. Minority residents only, near parity too. Pool them inside each ZIP and a gap shows up, because hosting ZIPs have more minority residents, and minority poverty runs higher nationally regardless of data centers. Real arithmetic, measuring composition, not harm.

Still no known site-opening dates, so an effect predating 2011 isn't ruled out. This piece covers the demographic half; the pollution half got its own historical test.
