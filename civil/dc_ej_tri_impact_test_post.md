**I went and got the EPA data. Here's the pollution-side test [until I get hired]**

Two pieces back: data-center ZIP codes score worse on environmental-justice burden, and that survives a density control. Last piece: the demographic half of that gap is mostly selection -- 87% already existed a decade before the recent boom. That piece flagged something explicitly: the *pollution* half of the EJ index had no historical data available to test the same way. So I went and got more.

Superfund and RCRA sites turned out to be dead ends -- no listing dates in the API fields the pipeline uses. But EPA's Toxics Release Inventory does support a year filter, letting me rebuild "distance to the nearest actively-reporting industrial facility" for 2013 vs. 2022, nationally, per ZIP.

Important limit up front: data centers themselves essentially never show up in TRI (it's chemical manufacturing, not IT/cooling). This can't test whether a data center directly polluted -- only whether the surrounding industrial neighborhood changed differently near data-center ZIPs.

It did, and more than I expected. Hosting ZIPs were already ~5x closer to active industrial facilities in 2013 (selection again, matching the demographic result). But unlike the demographic residual -- which was small, about 10% of the gap -- this one isn't: hosting ZIPs' distance to the nearest active facility shrank 1.29 km more than density- and baseline-matched non-hosting ZIPs over those 9 years, real against a permutation test.

Then a sharper question, and a real one: the quintile breakdown showed a much bigger effect in the two least-dense bins, but those only had 38 and 59 hosting ZIPs in them -- is that a real pattern, or just noise in thin bins? Checked properly two ways: bootstrap confidence intervals per quintile (one of five, the middle one, doesn't even individually clear zero), and a continuous regression that uses all 1,196 hosting ZIPs at once instead of splitting them into bins. That settles it: the interaction between hosting status and density is real (permutation p=0.005). The effect is concentrated almost entirely in rural/exurban data-center ZIPs (-2.1 km implied at low density) and is essentially zero in urban ones (+0.01 km implied at high density). Not a caveat on the finding -- a real refinement of it.

Two honest caveats keep this from being a "data centers cause industrial pollution" headline: it could be common-cause siting (both DCs and new industry drawn to the same cheap land/power/light zoning), or reverse causality (rising industrial activity could itself help attract a data center). This test can't tell those apart. What it does establish: something about the pollution side is moving differently near rural data-center ZIPs specifically, and that's worth its own follow-up.
